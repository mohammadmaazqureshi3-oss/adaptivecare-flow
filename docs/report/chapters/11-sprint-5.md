## Design

### Why simulate

The software-qualities slides say performance can be verified by "performance evaluation (on a
model, via simulation)". Real pathways have parallel steps, priorities, durations that are not
exponential, and arrivals that change by the hour. No closed formula covers that combination,
so the formula is used only where it is exact: to validate the simulator.

**Input — a scenario:** the pathway version with a duration distribution per task (lognormal,
triangular, exponential or fixed) and branch probabilities (DES-06); arrivals per hour of the day;
the urgency mix of arriving patients; staff per role and the cost weight of each role; deadline
targets such as "at most 5% of urgency 1–2 patients wait more than 5 minutes for confirmation";
the simulated length, the warm-up and the seed.

**Output:** for each role, utilisation, mean and 95th-percentile wait, and queue length; for each
deadline, the share missed; for each target, the share of patients beyond its limit; the length
of stay and the number of patients in the system — every figure with a 95% interval.

### Discrete-event simulation

{{uml U22}}

A replication keeps a future-event list ordered by time, with ties broken by insertion order.
**Arrivals** that change by the hour are generated by thinning [12]: candidates are drawn at the peak
rate and each is kept with probability rate(t)/peak. A new virtual case gets an urgency sampled from
the mix and is moved by `pathway-core`'s `TokenGraph`, the same token rules the engine runs.

A task's move is split in two (decision 0005): the token is taken when the work *starts* and put on
the next arrow when it *ends*. A human task asks its role's staff pool. If someone is free the work
starts now; otherwise the task waits in a non-preemptive priority queue ordered by (urgency,
arrival). Service and model tasks need no staff and take a sampled duration. A decision evaluates
its condition when every name it reads is sampled data (such as `urgency`), and otherwise draws a
branch by its probabilities. Statistics are collected after the warm-up: waits from request to
start of service, time-weighted utilisation and queue lengths, deadline misses, target shares,
length of stay, and the time-average number of patients in the system.

{{uml U25}}

### Statistics

Each metric is estimated from independent replications with different seeds and reported as

$$\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

Replications are added adaptively: 10 first, then 5 at a time, until every key metric's half-width
is at most 5% of its mean or below a small absolute tolerance (0.25 min for waits, 0.01 for
utilisation, 0.005 for shares), up to 100; if the cap is reached, the result says so. Without the
absolute tolerance, metrics near zero — the wait of an idle role — would never converge
(decision 0005). The first simulated day is discarded as warm-up, because the system starts empty.

### Staffing Advisor

{{uml U23}}

The advisor starts with one person per role. Each round runs 10 replications with **common random
numbers** — separate random streams for arrivals, work and routing, seeded per replication — so
that two staffings are compared on the same patients. If every target is met at the *upper* end of
its 95% interval, it recommends that staffing. Otherwise it tries one more person in each role and
adds the one with the largest reduction in total shortfall per unit of cost; if no single addition
helps, it adds to the busiest role (the bottleneck). It stops with "no staffing within the budget
meets every target" when the cost passes the budget. The recommendation is re-run adaptively before
it is shown. The search is a **greedy heuristic**: it finds a good staffing, not a proven optimum,
so an exhaustive search on a small case measures the gap.

{{uml U24}}

### Validation

- **M/M/c against Erlang C.** With one role and exponential arrivals and durations, the exact mean
  wait is given by the Erlang C formula (chapter 2). For λ = 8 per hour, mean service 12 minutes
  and *c* = 2, *W<sub>q</sub>* = 21.33 minutes. Across 100 independent experiments the formula's value
  must fall inside the simulated 95% interval about 95 times; 90 or more passes.
- **Little's law**: the average number in the system equals the arrival rate times the average
  time in the system, within the interval.
- **Extreme cases**: no arrivals gives no waits; one nurse under an arrival rate above capacity gives
  waits that keep growing with the run length.
- **Determinism**: the same seed gives identical results.

### Undeploy (DEP-02)

The deployer calls the engine's `POST /drain`; the engine stores the flag and ignores its start
trigger; a watcher checks `running_cases` every 2 s and stops the engine at zero. The version moves
Deployed → Draining → Retired, only forwards (diagram U07). Running cases always finish on the
version they started on, which is why versions are immutable.

## Implementation notes

`simulator/des.py` (simulation), `stats.py` (replications and intervals), `advisor.py` (greedy and
exhaustive searches), `queueing.py` (Erlang C, used only for validation), `scenario.py` (scenarios,
targets, the readiness check of DES-06). The simulator service runs jobs in background threads and
stores scenarios and results; the Simulate page shows every figure with its interval, utilisation
bars and the advice.

## AI usage

1. **A test that proved nothing.** The first priority-queue test checked only that urgent patients
   were served at all, in a hopelessly overloaded queue. It was rewritten to compare mean waits by
   urgency in a busy but stable queue (utilisation 0.9).
2. **Lost bookkeeping.** The first version recorded each case's urgency by wrapping a method after
   the fact and lost cases that completed instantly; urgency is now recorded at arrival.
3. **A wrong node count** (19, not 20) in the NFR-PERF-03 test pathway: the test was fixed, so the
   pathway has the 20 nodes the NFR names.
